Zone names, % HRR ranges, and RPE ranges come from the ACSM classification of exercise intensity (Garber et al., 2011). RPE uses the Borg 6–20 scale (Borg, 1982): 7 extremely light, 9 very light, 11 light, 13 somewhat hard, 15 hard, 17 very hard, 19 extremely hard. Percent HRR and RPE track each other across groups, but the match varies from person to person, so use RPE as a cross-check on heart rate, especially when medication blunts heart rate.

Equations and limits

Tanaka: HRmax = 208 − 0.7 × age, derived from a meta-analysis and cross-validated in healthy adults (Tanaka et al., 2001). Brawner: HRmax = 164 − 0.7 × age, derived in patients with coronary heart disease taking beta-blockers; r² = 0.13 with a standard error of the estimate of 18 bpm (Brawner et al., 2004). Karvonen: target HR = (HRmax − HRrest) × intensity + HRrest (Karvonen et al., 1957).

Age-based equations are population estimates, and individual HRmax can differ substantially from the predicted value. Brawner is not intended for people who are not taking a beta-blocker, and it does not apply to heart failure, which has its own equation (Keteyian et al., 2012). A measured peak heart rate from a symptom-limited exercise test is preferred whenever one is available, and heart rate should be paired with RPE and symptom monitoring.

Intensity zones: The zone names, % HRR ranges, and matching RPE ranges follow the ACSM classification of exercise intensity for apparently healthy adults (Garber et al., 2011). They are not specific to older adults, people with cardiovascular disease, or people on beta-blockers.

How to read these values

Equations and limits

MAP: DBP + (SBP − DBP) ÷ 3, which equals (SBP + 2 × DBP) ÷ 3 (DeMers and Wachs, StatPearls). This is an estimate from cuff readings. MAP is most accurately obtained from an arterial catheter, and an inaccurate blood pressure reading gives an inaccurate MAP. The usual reference range shown here is 70 to 100 mmHg, although some sources extend it to about 110. A MAP of about 60 mmHg is commonly cited as the minimum needed to perfuse vital organs. For adults with septic shock on vasopressors, the Surviving Sepsis Campaign recommends an initial MAP target of 65 mmHg (Evans et al., 2021).

Pulse pressure: SBP − DBP. About 40 mmHg is typical at rest in healthy young adults. A pulse pressure below 25% of the systolic pressure is considered inappropriately narrow, which can occur with low stroke volume states such as heart failure, blood loss, and aortic stenosis. Pulse pressure usually rises with age as arteries stiffen (Homan et al., StatPearls).

Rate pressure product: HR × SBP, also called the double product. It is a noninvasive index of myocardial oxygen demand that correlated with measured myocardial oxygen consumption during exercise in patients with angina (Gobel et al., 1978). Compare values within the same person and under similar conditions, such as before and after an intervention or across exercise stages. This tool does not label an RPP as normal or abnormal, because no single cutoff applies to everyone, and the value at which ischemia appears varies between people and situations. RPP is often written in units of 10² (for example, 22,500 as 225 × 10²). When you enter a second reading, the comparison table reports the change in each measure as reading 2 minus reading 1.
